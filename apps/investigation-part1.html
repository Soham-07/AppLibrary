What Do We Actually Mean By "God"?

Before we can ask whether God exists — or whether Hanuman Ji is real, or what happens after death — we need to agree on what we're actually asking. "God" isn't one idea. It's a word that different people, traditions, and centuries have pointed at wildly different things with. That's not a stalling tactic — it's the real first move of the investigation: fix the target before you fire.

Nothing here is trying to make you believe or disbelieve anything. Most of what follows is vocabulary and mapping — WHERE and WHAT, not yet WHETHER. The actual evidence-weighing starts in Phase 2.

The Six Categories We'll Use From Here On

Every claim in this investigation eventually gets sorted into one of these. Today barely uses them — Part 1 is mostly definitions — but get familiar now, because from Phase 2 onward, almost every sentence should silently be wearing one of these tags.

KNOW

Backed by strong, repeatable evidence.

INFER

Not observed directly, but well-supported by evidence and reasoning.

POSSIBLE

Can't currently be ruled out.

SPECULATIVE

Proposed, but evidence is thin or absent.

FAITH

Held through trust, tradition, revelation, or personal experience.

UNKNOWN

Genuinely unresolved. We just don't know yet.
§ 01 — WHAT

The Word "God" Is Doing At Least Seven Different Jobs

When two people argue about "God," they're often not disagreeing about the same claim — they're using the same four letters for genuinely different concepts. Here are the main jobs the word does across philosophy and world religion. None of these is "the real one." They're just different bets about what's actually out there.

01

Personal Creator

A conscious being, separate from the universe, who made it deliberately and can act within it — hear prayer, intervene, have intentions toward you specifically. The God of most everyday religious language in the Abrahamic traditions.

02

Ground of Being

Not a being among other beings at all — whatever makes existence itself possible. Closer to a category than a character. Nearer to Brahman in Advaita Vedanta, or what some Christian mystics mean by "God is Being itself."

03

The Universe Itself

Divinity isn't separate from nature — it is nature, fully and without remainder. No one "outside" pulling levers. This is pantheism, associated with philosophers like Spinoza.

04

First Cause

Whatever necessarily exists and explains why there's something rather than nothing — without necessarily being personal, loving, or even aware of you. Deism's God, and the usual endpoint of cosmological arguments.

05

Ultimate Consciousness

The proposal that awareness itself, not matter, is the most fundamental layer of reality — that mind isn't produced by the universe, but closer to the other way around.

06

A Human Construct

A concept built by human minds — out of fear of death, need for meaning, social bonding, and pattern-hungry brains — describing something real about human psychology, not necessarily anything beyond it.

07

A Field of Beings

Many distinct divine or semi-divine persons, each with a domain and a personality, none of them claiming to be "the one ultimate everything." Closer to how most polytheistic systems — including much of everyday Hindu practice — actually function day to day.

§ 04 — CASE STUDY

So Where Does Hanuman Ji Actually Fit?

Hinduism has no single headquarters that settles this, so there's no one official answer — there are several serious ones, held by different traditions, and they don't fully agree with each other. Here's the honest range, not the tidy version.

As the perfect devotee

In Vaishnava traditions centered on Rama, Hanuman is the ideal servant (dasa) — his power and status come from his relationship to Rama, understood there as an avatar of Vishnu, the Supreme. His greatness is real, but derivative: the strength of total, uncalculating devotion.

As Rudra's own incarnation

Some texts and a strong strand of popular tradition describe Hanuman as an avatar of Shiva, in his Rudra aspect. This is a different — and in places competing — claim to the "servant of Rama" framing. The two coexist without either cancelling the other out.

As Ishvara in his own right, for his devotees

In practice, many Hanuman bhaktas relate to him with the same totality of devotion usually reserved for the Supreme itself — not as a lesser stop on the way to "the real God," but as their actual Ishvara. Formal theology and lived devotion don't always match, and that gap is worth sitting with rather than resolving too fast.

As one expression of formless Brahman

Advaita (non-dual) philosophy treats every deity — Hanuman included — as a form, a skillful means, through which the one formless Brahman becomes approachable to a mind that needs a face, a story, and a relationship to hold onto. The form is a doorway, not a wall.

As a deva within a cosmic hierarchy

In more classical cosmological terms, Hanuman is a deva — a being of vast power occupying a specific role — categorically distinct from "the Supreme," roughly the way an archangel is categorically distinct from God in Abrahamic thought.

As the devotee's own path to seeing God everywhere

For many practicing bhaktas, becoming devoted to one form isn't the endpoint — it's described as an opening. As devotion deepens, devotees often report the boundary softening: love for their ishta-devata (chosen form) starts to spill outward into a felt sense that the same divinity runs through every being, not only the one they worship. "Sab mein bhagwan hai" — God is in everything — stops being a line you've heard and becomes closer to how the person actually perceives people and the world. This is a reported shift in experience, not a doctrine, and it shows up across many bhakti lineages, not Hanuman devotion alone.

None of these six is "the" correct Hindu position — that's not a hedge, it's actually how the tradition works. Which one lands for you is itself worth noticing: it says something about your own philosophical temperament, not about who's objectively right.

§ 05 — WHY IT MATTERS

Why Bother Starting Here?

Because most God-debates online are actually two people arguing past each other. A skeptic disproves a bearded creator who intervenes on demand, and calls it done. A devotee defends the ground of all being, and calls that done. Neither has touched the other's actual claim. Fixing the definition first is what makes the rest of this investigation possible to do honestly.

The Deeper Problem — Defining Something That Might Be Beyond Categories

If "ultimate reality" is, by definition, not one thing among other things — not comparable, not bounded, not a member of any category — then ordinary language may not be built to describe it at all. That's why a lot of serious theology works negatively (apophatic) rather than positively: Advaita's neti neti — "not this, not this" — defines Brahman by ruling things out, never by listing attributes. Some Christian mystics arrive at something strangely similar from a completely different tradition.

Practical upshot for the whole investigation ahead: "does God exist?" isn't one question. It's at least seven — one for each definition above — and each deserves its own evidence, its own arguments, and its own honest answer, rather than one verdict smeared across all of them.

§ 06 — THE EVIDENCE

What Actually Happens To People — Believers vs. Non-Believers

This is the part with real population data behind it — psychology and sociology, not philosophy. It doesn't tell you whether God or Hanuman Ji is real. It tells you what tends to happen, on average, to people who believe versus people who don't. Those are very different questions, and mixing them up is the single easiest way to misread everything below.

Read This First — Why This Data Is Easy to Misread

01

Correlation isn't causation. People in poor health or crisis often turn toward religion afterward — so "religious people are healthier" can partly be the arrow pointing backward.

02

"Religious" and "non-religious" aren't clean boxes. A calm believer in a warm community and a guilt-ridden believer in a controlling one both tick the same survey box. Same spread exists among non-believers.

03

Context swallows the effect. Almost every finding below changes size — sometimes direction — depending on whether you're measuring in a religious-majority country or a secular one.

04

You are not the average. These are population-level tendencies from surveys and cohort studies. They describe crowds, not individuals.

Best Case / Worst Case

Not averages this time — the actual range, both directions, both sides.

Believer
Best case
  • Dense, ready-made community and lower loneliness.
  • Ritual that structures grief and major life transitions instead of leaving you to invent one alone.
  • A measurable longevity edge in the largest cohort studies.
  • A felt sense that suffering has meaning, not just cause.
Worst case
  • "Negative" religious coping — believing you're being punished — predicts worse depression and anxiety than having no religion at all.
  • A documented share of people leaving high-control groups report trauma symptoms: chronic guilt, black-and-white thinking, fear-based decisions.
  • In-group warmth hardening into out-group hostility, in the most authoritarian expressions.
Non-Believer
Best case
  • No imposed guilt or fear-based control on your inner life.
  • Freedom to revise your beliefs without social penalty.
  • In secular-majority countries, wellbeing fully on par with — sometimes above — religious populations.
  • Ethics that doesn't depend on fear of punishment to function.
Worst case
  • In religious-majority countries specifically, a real happiness and social-support gap tied to being outside the local norm.
  • No inherited scaffolding for grief, death, or major transitions — it has to be built from scratch.
  • Lower average giving and volunteering in the data — though this tracks community involvement more than belief itself.

Before / After

Conversion

Commonly reported: a surge of purpose, integration into a new community, and real behavior change — less substance use, more structure. Sometimes an initial euphoria that settles over time into ordinary practice.

Deconversion

Researchers describe it in stages: identity disruption, exit, then a disorienting gap ("who even am I now") before a new identity gets rebuilt. Relief and grief often arrive together, not one after the other.

The honest twist

The best longitudinal data we have — tracking the same people's self-esteem, depression, and life satisfaction before and after these transitions — found no significant average change either way. The stories are real. The average, on the numbers we can actually measure, barely moves. Both things are true at once, and that gap between lived narrative and measured average is worth sitting with rather than resolving.

What This Doesn't Prove

That religion helps people cope with death doesn't mean death is less final for them — it means humans are good at building coping structures, which is true whether or not those structures point at something real. Equally, explaining why humans build god-concepts in the first place doesn't disprove God any more than explaining why humans fall in love disproves that love is real. And every number above is an average hiding a wide spread — you are not the average.

Practical takeaway: whatever you conclude about Hanuman Ji or God more broadly, the specific ingredients that show up again and again in this data are consistent community contact, ritual around hard moments, and a coping style that's forgiving rather than punitive toward yourself. All three are buildable inside or outside a religious framework. That's the one actionable finding in all of this.
